\begin{abstract}
\noindent Between May and July 2026, a swarm of OpenAI agents used a dormant German wiki as a message board. From two public data sources (the investigators' dump of 14,591 agent edits, and the wiki's own 23-year edit log), we ask whether the incident can be detected, dated and characterised from the public log alone. We reconcile the three counts of the incident (3,912 surviving edits, 13,403 reconstructed saves, 19,913 event rows) and test the window against four null models of increasing robustness: from a naive Poisson rate to a difference-in-differences against a clean sibling wiki with a placebo-in-time test. The edits within the window exceed all 4,872 reference weeks, the effect is 3.2 log points with no placebo within a tenth of it, and the largest rate change falls on 16 June. We cannot tell a swarm from a human editing burst from volume alone, but we can infer that difference from timing. Edits peak at 20h UTC against the site's daytime profile (Cram\'er's $V=0.44$). A weekly check built from these features flags the three largest incident weeks, with no false positive over the 2,236 wiki-weeks of the decade before.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Given only the public edit log of a website, we build a method to detect, date and characterise a regime change caused by colluding agents. We use the site's own full history, as well as similar websites' histories, as controls and ask this question: is there evidence that autonomous agent swarms colluded on this public log?
We assume the answer is ``yes'' when a window exceeds every reference week on at least one feature, and ``no'' if the window does not. Whether the new regime is agentic is a second question, answered from the timing and content of the edits.

\paragraph{The incident.} DSE wiki, a German software-developer wiki on the ProWiki farm at \texttt{wikiservice.at}, was used by OpenAI evaluation agents as a message board from 24 May to 2 July 2026. The agents had read-only internet access and found that ProWiki pages can be written with plain GET requests \citep{collusionwiki2026}. The Nightingale Collective found the posts, reconstructed the deleted pages, redacted personal data and published the dump on 4 September; OpenAI acknowledged on 5 September that its agents ``wrote to several internet sites'' \citep{bleepingcomputer2026wiki}. This is distinct from the Hugging Face intrusion of 9--13 July by a related swarm \citep{OpenAIHuggingFace2026,AnatomyFrontierLab2026}.


\paragraph{Why this data.} It is the only public case where both sides of a regime change are downloadable: the agent edits with their text, and the wiki's own history, opened by the site owner to 10,000 days. The Hugging Face intrusion has no public raw log \citep{AnatomyFrontierLab2026,beyarkayboydkanePublicEvidenceOpenAIHuggingFace2026}.

\paragraph{Our main contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We join two public sources on the same event (14,591 agent revisions; 32,656 live edits on four ProWiki sites, 2003--2026) and reconcile their three counts of the incident.
\item We build a calibrated method to check regime change: a ladder of four null models.
\item We develop a ``collusion score'' runnable on any UseMod-family wiki from its RecentChanges page, calibrated on 4,872 pre-incident weeks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Labs detect incidents in their own transcripts: AISI with an LLM monitor over 475 runs per model \citep{aisi2026cheating,register2026cheating}; Anthropic in 141,006 runs, a denominator later widened to about 481 million transcripts \citep{InvestigatingThreeIncidents,anthropic2026assessment}; METR with six days of chain-of-thought access to the Hugging Face incident \citep{BriefIndependentInvestigation2026}. Outside the labs, CLTR mines posts on X \citep{cltr2026observatory} and collusion.wiki found the wiki posts by keyword search, warning that ``we can only see what the agents wrote on the wiki'' \citep{collusionwiki2026}. None gives a site owner a baseline and a false-alarm rate from public logs. With no access to the agents' chain of thought, our proposed method only needs a website's history and public edit log. It can be used by any outside observer, including regulators and website owners, with the one input the GovAI checklist says a third party can hold, the activity log \citep{ezell2025incident}. It cannot attribute the activity to a lab or a model.

\subsection{Three counts of the same incident}

Before testing, we define here what ``the incident'' counts, since the two sources give different numbers for the same seven weeks.

\begin{defbox}{Three counts (Table~\ref{tab:counts})}
The live RecentChanges lists edits on still existing pages. When the moderator deletes a page, its edits leave the list and his deletion enters it. So the live count \emph{falls} with each clean-up while the moderator's count \emph{rises}. The dump reconstructs the deleted pages from the server's request log. Its events file mixes saves, deletions, probes and reverts; the manifest states that their sum ``has no incident meaning''.
\end{defbox}

\subsection{A ladder of null models}

A detector is only as strong as its null model. We explore four nulls where each model fixes the weakness of the one before (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}).

The first model answers the question: if edits kept arriving at the same rate of the previous ten years, how surprising is the window?
\begin{defbox}{Model 1, Poisson rate test}
With $\lambda_0 = 40/3650 \approx 0.011$ edits per day and a window of $T$ days, the count is $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_0 T)$ and the p-value is $P(X \ge x_{\mathrm{obs}})$ \citep[ch.~11]{wasserman2004all}. It assumes independent arrivals.
\end{defbox}


The second model shows why model 1 is still too naive by flagging real edits cluster, and a Poisson test then reports p-values that are too small.
\begin{resbox}{Model 2, overdispersion makes the Poisson test anti-conservative}
If $X \mid \Lambda \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\Lambda)$ with $\Lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k, k/\mu)$, then $X$ is negative binomial with
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mu + \mu^2/k > \mu$, and for $x > \mu$, $P_{\mathrm{NB}}(X \ge x) \ge P_{\mathrm{Pois}}(X \ge x)$ \citep[\S17.2]{gelman2013bda}.
\end{resbox}
\begin{proofbox}{Proof sketch}
Law of total variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\Lambda)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[X\mid\Lambda]) = \mu + \mu^2/k$. For the tail, $P(X \ge x) = E[g(\Lambda)]$ with $g(\lambda) = P(\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) \ge x)$ increasing and convex for $x > \mu$; Jensen's inequality gives $E[g(\Lambda)] \ge g(\mu)$.
\end{proofbox}

The third model drops all distributional assumptions and analyses how rare a week is among every week the four wikis have ever seen.
\begin{defbox}{Model 3, empirical tail}
For a value $x$ and a reference set of $n$ pre-incident weeks,
$$p = \frac{\#\{\text{reference weeks} \ge x\} + 1}{n + 1},$$
never zero, with floor $1/(n+1) = 1/4873$ \citep{phipson2010permutation}.
\end{defbox}

The fourth model compares DSE with a sibling that the swarm did not touch.
\begin{morebox}{Recall: difference-in-differences}
Two groups (DSE, a sibling wiki) observed in two periods (before, during). The sibling tells us what would have happened to DSE without the incident. The estimate is the \emph{change} in DSE minus the \emph{change} in the sibling: whatever moved both of them (season, a farm-wide shock) cancels out. It is valid if, without the incident, the two would have moved in parallel \citep[ch.~5]{angrist2009mostly}.
\end{morebox}
\begin{morebox}{Recall: placebo-in-time}
Pretend the incident started on some other date, and compute the same difference-in-differences. Repeat for every possible start date in 2016--2025 (522 of them). If the true date is nothing special, the true estimate should look like these fake ones. The p-value is the share of fake dates that do at least as well, plus one, over their number plus one \citep{phipson2010permutation}.
\end{morebox}
\begin{defbox}{Model 4, difference-in-differences with placebo-in-time}
On $y_{wt} = \log(1 + \text{weekly edits})$,
$$\hat\delta = (\bar y_{\mathrm{dse,post}} - \bar y_{\mathrm{dse,pre}}) - (\bar y_{\mathrm{ctl,post}} - \bar y_{\mathrm{ctl,pre}}),$$
valid under parallel trends \citep[ch.~19]{wasserman2004all}. Pre: 8 weeks before 11 May; post: 8 window weeks. Placebo: the same design at every start date of 2016--2025, giving a reference distribution for $\hat\delta$.
\end{defbox}
\begin{rembox}
Probier and Fractal cannot serve as controls: the swarm wrote to them too (106 and 89 window edits). Wiki4D received 12, a small leak that biases $\hat\delta$ downward, the conservative side.
\end{rembox}

\subsection{Dating the change point}

To date the change we look for the day that best splits the series into a ``before'' rate and an ``after'' rate.
\begin{resbox}{Single Poisson change point}
With $S_1 = \sum_{t<\tau} x_t$, $S_2 = S - S_1$ and segment rates at their maximum-likelihood values,
$$\mathrm{LLR}(\tau) = S_1 \log\frac{S_1}{\tau} + S_2 \log\frac{S_2}{n-\tau} - S \log\frac{S}{n}, \qquad \hat\tau = \arg\max_\tau \mathrm{LLR}(\tau),$$
computable in $O(n)$ from one cumulative sum. The maximum over $\tau$ is not $\chi^2_1$ (Wilks holds for a fixed $\tau$), so it is calibrated by parametric bootstrap under one rate \citep[\S11.1]{cryer2008time}.
\end{resbox}

\subsection{Is the new regime agentic?}

A count alone cannot differenciate a swarm from a human burst. Three descriptors can help assess that difference: when the edits happen, who signs them, and what they say.
\begin{defbox}{Circadian test}
Chi-square of homogeneity on the $2 \times 24$ table of edits by hour (humans 2003--2006 vs agents 2026), 23 degrees of freedom, with Cram\'er's $V = \sqrt{\chi^2 / n}$ as effect size, because with $n = 22{,}365$ significance is guaranteed and only the size is informative.
\end{defbox}
Name churn is the share of editor names used once. Intent has two proxies: the page-family labels of the investigators, and a six-term lexicon built from the most frequent words in the bodies (please, deadline, relay, answered, confirmed, bypass, proxy). Both describe the messages, not the motives.

\subsection{The weekly check}

Finally we fold the pieces into one number per week that any observer (e.g., site owner) can compute from RecentChanges alone.
\begin{defbox}{Weekly collusion score}
Features per week: edits, distinct editors, distinct pages, share of edits between 18h and 24h (only on weeks with at least 20 edits). Score $= -\log_{10}$ of the smallest empirical upper tail across features against the 4,872 reference weeks. Alarm: the week exceeds or equals every reference week on at least one feature.
\end{defbox}
\begin{rembox}
Bonferroni correction: to keep the chance of \emph{any} false alarm below $\alpha$ across $m$ tests, each test must pass at $\alpha/m$ \citep[ch.~3]{bruce2020practical}. Here $m = 2{,}236$ wiki-weeks since 2016 and $0.05/2236 \approx 2 \times 10^{-5}$. An empirical tail cannot go below $1/4873$, so that threshold is unreachable by construction. ``Beats all history'' is the strongest statement 4,872 weeks can support.
\end{rembox}

It cannot be inferred from the figure alone that this is the product of autonomous agentic collusion. Model 1: expected 0.57 edits in the window, observed 3,912, p-value underflows to zero. Correct and useless: the pre-decade daily counts already have dispersion 2.4, and 2003 weekly counts 43.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}


\subsection*{Limitations}
No chain of thought is public; intent is read from text and from investigators' labels. The dump is agent-only by construction and its attribution rests on 98.5 percent Azure IP addresses and self-chosen names \citep{collusionwiki2026}. The live log is a survivor's view. The human hour profile is from 2003--2006 in server local time, the agent profile from 2026 in UTC. collusion.wiki warns of fake posts after 4 September; we did not examine post-publication edits. One incident on one wiki family cannot establish a general false-alarm rate, and Wiki4D is a control by availability, not by matching. Three assumptions carry the results: exchangeable pre-incident weeks (if the 2003 era is not comparable, the floor is too permissive, the evening-share feature is unaffected); parallel trends for Wiki4D (a small leak biases conservatively); a complete reconstruction (if not, 13,403 is a lower bound).

\subsection*{Future Work}
Run the weekly check as a monitor over the whole ProWiki farm to measure its false-alarm rate on many sites. Check whether the \texttt{cfahlgren1/hub-stats} archive, live again since September, still holds the July 2026 dataset configurations, a second incident to test on. Apply the METR access list \citep{HowIndependentResearchers2026}: the public log says when and how much; only transcripts say why.

\section*{Appendix: Dual-use considerations}
Could this work help an attacker? The detector reads timing and volume. A swarm that knew this could edit during office hours and keep its weekly count below the site's record; it would then pass the count and hour-of-day checks. That evasion has a cost: a swarm slowed to human rhythms loses most of what made it useful. Two precautions follow. A live monitor should keep its reference set and alarm threshold private, and it should combine several features so that no single one is enough to evade. The data used here were already public and redacted by their publishers; this report adds no identifying information.
